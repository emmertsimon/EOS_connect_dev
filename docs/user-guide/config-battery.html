Battery & inverter

The store the optimizer plans around, the hardware it drives, and the running price of what is inside it.

What this is for

  • I want EOS Connect to know my battery — how big it is, how fast it charges, how far it may be emptied.
  • I want it to actually drive my inverter rather than only advise me.
  • I want to know what the energy sitting in the battery cost me, so a discharge decision has a price behind it.

Two settings groups: battery.* describes the store and what it contains, inverter.* is how EOS Connect talks to the hardware.

The battery itself

Capacity, the charge and discharge limits, and the state of charge the optimizer plans against. These are the numbers every plan is built on, so a capacity that is out by a third makes every decision out by a third.

How fast the battery charges when it is nearly full

No battery takes its full charge power all the way to 100 %: the BMS tapers it as the cells fill. With the charging curve on, EOS Connect lowers the charge limit it writes to the inverter as the state of charge rises, and the built-in optimizer plans every slot with the power the battery will really take at the state of charge it reaches there — so a plan that needs the battery full by evening starts early enough.

Without points the built-in curve is used: full power up to 50 %, then a steady taper to 0.05 C at the top. Most home batteries hold full power much longer than that. If yours does, enter its curve as points — state of charge against power — in battery.charging_curve_points, for example

0:6000, 90:6000, 95:3000, 100:800

Power is in watts, or in percent of the maximum charge power (90:100%, 100:15%); kW works too. Between two points the power is interpolated in a straight line, before the first and after the last it is held flat, so you only need the points where something changes. Write a decimal comma? Then separate the points with semicolons or line breaks.

The battery temperature derating applies on top, to the live limit and to the plan alike. The plan reads the curve at each slot's average state of charge. A curve whose power falls off ever more steeply towards full (a concave curve) is planned exactly and costs nothing. A curve that drops hard and then levels off into a long tail bends upward; by default the plan follows its envelope there, which keeps the solve as fast as without a curve but lets the plan expect the last few percent sooner than they arrive — the log says by how much. eos.local_evopt_exact_charging_curve plans such a curve exactly instead, at the price of solves that take seconds rather than a fraction of one. Up to three stretches are kept as they are; more are merged where it costs the least charging time, and a merged stretch is planned slightly under the curve, never above it. The exact rounds re-plan battery and grid with managed loads held where the first solve placed them, and share a time budget (four times the first solve, at least 5 s): should they run out, the envelope plan stands and the log says so. The live limit always follows the points exactly. The external EVopt service and the EOS server take one fixed maximum: with those only the live limit follows the curve.

What your stored energy cost

EOS Connect can track a running price for the energy in the battery, from what it actually paid to put it there — grid energy at that hour's tariff, solar at the export you gave up. Discharging then has a cost to weigh against the current tariff instead of being treated as free. See What your stored energy actually cost in the User Guide for what it is worth in practice.
